What a Scan Report Actually Proves

By Christian Ferrier, CPWA · 15 August 2026

A scan report lands on your desk: 312 accessibility violations on your client's website, generated by an automated testing tool. (The figure is illustrative; nothing below depends on it.) That count carries the argument. It suggests a broadly inaccessible site, a defendant who ignored the problem, and a settlement to match.

In almost every case, the number is not what it appears to be. The tool did not malfunction, and nobody is being dishonest. The gap comes from what automated testing can measure and how the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines are built. Three properties of these reports inflate the count. Each is testable, and each can be examined before anyone negotiates a settlement figure.

Not all of WCAG is a requirement

WCAG is usually described as though it were a single body of rules. It is not. The W3C divides it into normative and informative material, and the division is written into the standard itself. It is not a matter of interpretation.

The normative parts are the success criteria — the numbered testable statements such as 1.1.1 Non-text Content or 2.4.7 Focus Visible — together with the conformance requirements and the glossary definitions the criteria rely on. Conformance to WCAG means satisfying the success criteria. That is the whole of it.

The supporting material the W3C publishes alongside it — the Understanding documents and the Techniques — is informative. The W3C states this in terms that leave little room for argument:

It goes further and cautions explicitly against requiring them. The W3C then states the point that matters most here: if content does not implement a sufficient technique, it does not necessarily fail the success criterion. There are other ways to satisfy a criterion, and using none of the documented techniques is one of them.

One part of the supporting material works differently and should not be lumped in. The W3C also publishes documented Failures, and a documented failure is a condition that does fail a specific success criterion. Where a finding maps to one of those, it is on solid ground. The distinction is between “this did not implement a recommended technique” and “this matches a documented failure condition” — the first supports no violation, the second supports one.

The parts of WCAG, and whether each is normative
Part of WCAG Type Testable on its own?
Principles (Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust) Normative No — organizing categories only
Guidelines (13 total) Normative No — general goals under each principle
Success criteria (e.g. 1.1.1, 2.4.7) Normative Yes — this is what conformance actually means
Techniques (Sufficient/Advisory) Informative No — one way to meet a criterion, not the only way
Understanding documents Informative No — explains intent, adds no requirement
Documented Failures Informative label, but functions as binding Yes — a documented Failure means a criterion is unsatisfied

This matters because automated testing tools are largely built on the informative layer. A rule engine cannot evaluate “the purpose of each link can be determined from the link text” — that requires reading the page and knowing what the link does. What it can evaluate is a proxy: whether the link text is one of a list of known-vague strings, whether an image has an alt attribute, whether an element carries a particular ARIA role. Those proxies are usually good ones. But a proxy failing is not the same as a success criterion being unsatisfied, and a report that lists both under a single heading called “violations” has merged two different claims.

In practice, this means a finding that rests only on an unimplemented technique, with no showing that the underlying success criterion is unsatisfied, does not establish nonconformance. It may still deserve a fix. It is not a violation, and it should not be counted as one.

One defect, counted many times

The second property is arithmetic. Automated tools crawl pages and report findings per page, per instance. That is the correct behavior for a development tool — an engineer fixing a site wants to see every location. It produces a badly misleading number in a legal document.

Modern websites are assembled from shared components. A single navigation menu, header, cookie banner, or product-card template is written once and rendered on every page. If that one component has an accessibility defect, a crawl of one hundred pages reports one hundred violations. There is one defect. There is one fix. Fixing it once resolves all one hundred findings simultaneously.

This is why the raw count in a scan report says so little about the scale of the underlying problem or the cost of fixing it. A report listing 312 findings may describe eight distinct defects, six of them in shared components. That picture differs materially from the one the number suggests, and the difference bears directly on remediation cost and on the plaintiff's characterization of the site.

The correct unit is the distinct defect, with the number of instances recorded separately. Two findings are the same defect when they share a root cause and one correction resolves both. Determining that requires looking at the site rather than the report. It is not difficult, and it is often the single largest reduction available.

Some findings do not reproduce

The third property is the one tool vendors are most open about. Automated testing cannot evaluate context, and the better tools say so in their own documentation and interfaces.

Two categories need separating. The first is findings the tool flags but cannot resolve — axe reports these as “incomplete” or “needs review,” and WAVE calls them “alerts.” These are explicitly requests for a human to look. They are not determinations. When they are aggregated into a violation count, they are being presented as something their own vendor says they are not.

The second is findings the tool states affirmatively but which do not survive testing. An image flagged as missing alternative text may be decorative and correctly hidden from assistive technology. A contrast failure may be computed against a background the tool guessed at incorrectly. A form field flagged as unlabeled may be labeled by a mechanism the tool did not follow. In each case the rule fired correctly on the information available to it, and the conclusion is still wrong.

None of this is a criticism of the tools, and their reach deserves accuracy rather than dismissal. Deque, which publishes the axe engine underlying much of the industry, reports that automated testing covered 57 percent of the accessibility issues found across some two thousand audits. It advances that figure against the lower numbers often quoted, and it arrives at it by counting issues rather than success criteria. Automation finds a great deal.

What it does not do is establish how many distinct defects exist, whether each finding survives inspection, or whether a criterion is actually unsatisfied. Those are different questions, and a scan answers none of them. The problem is not the tool. It is that output designed to direct a developer's attention gets repackaged as a measure of the extent of a legal violation.

What manual testing establishes

Testing the allegation list means taking the findings as alleged and examining each one on the live site with a keyboard and a screen reader, in a recorded environment, with reproduction steps. For each alleged item it produces one of four answers:

  • It reproduces, and the normative text of a success criterion is not satisfied. This is a genuine violation, and it is reported as one.
  • It reproduces as a real barrier, but no success criterion addresses it. A fix is warranted; a violation it is not, and it is reported separately as advisory.
  • It is the same defect as another item on the list, with an instance count.
  • It does not hold up, with the specific reason — the rule misapplied, the context the tool could not evaluate, or the condition it could not detect.

The result is a matrix covering every item alleged, written to be handed to the other side. It does not soften findings. Where a barrier is real, it says so plainly, and identifying real defects early is generally to the defendant's advantage — remediation is cheaper than litigation, and a documented, completed remediation with a policy preventing recurrence has supported mootness arguments in web accessibility cases.

What it does is replace an unexamined number with an examined one.

A note on the standard itself

One further point deserves precision, because it is frequently blurred in both directions.

The ADA does not, by its terms, require WCAG conformance for private businesses, and as of this writing no Title III regulation adopts it. The Department of Justice did adopt WCAG 2.1 Level AA by rule in 2024, but that rule reaches state and local government entities under Title II — 89 Fed. Reg. 31320 (Apr. 24, 2024), codified at 28 C.F.R. § 35.200. Courts have nonetheless widely treated WCAG 2.x Level AA as a workable benchmark, and it appears routinely in settlements and consent decrees. For a private business the statutory question remains effective access.

The technical consequence cuts both ways. A conformance failure is evidence of an access problem, not proof of one — a criterion can be unsatisfied in a way no user ever encounters. And a genuine barrier can exist with no conformance failure attached, because WCAG does not reach everything, particularly in cognitive accessibility. A technical analysis that reports only one of those two things is incomplete.

What this is not

This is a technical analysis, not a legal one. Whether a given barrier constitutes a denial of access under the ADA, whether an alternative was reasonably achievable, and what any of it is worth are questions for counsel. An examiner who answers them has stepped outside their competence, and an opposing party will say so.

The scope of the technical work is narrower and, precisely because it is narrow, harder to attack: this is what the site does, this is the environment it was tested in, these are the steps to reproduce it, and this is the requirement that is or is not satisfied. Anyone can repeat it and get the same answer.
